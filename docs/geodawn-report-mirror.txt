Introduction 
 
Task Order Task Order No. 140G0221F0024 NV_WestCentral_Geophysical_2020_D21 was 
issued in March of 2021 to NV5 Geospatial, Inc. and its sub- contractor EDCON-PRJ, Inc. The 
GeoDAWN Survey is one of many geophysical surveys recently funded through the Earth 
Mapping Resources Initiative (Earth MRI). The contract cover ed the acquisition and processing 
of aeromagnetic and radiometric data acquired over a portion of west-central Nevada (Figure 1).  
 
 
Figure 1. Location Map of the GeoDAWN Project Area. 
 
The GeoDAWN survey area encompasses 51,857 square kilometers in west -central Nevada. It 
includes portions of Churchill, Esmeralda, Eureka, Lander, Lyon, Mineral, Nye, Pershing, and 
Washoe Counties,  as well as small portions of Mono and Inyo Counties in California. The survey 
extends 330 kilometers from west to east and 370 kilometers from north to south. 


4 
 
A total of 149,030 linear kilometers of h igh-resolution aeromagnetic and radiometric data was 
acquired for the purpose of imaging the subsurface geology and structure of the area and to aid in 
identifying potential locations of undiscovered mineral and geothermal resources. These data will 
be used to evaluate the potential for undiscovered resources and will contribute to USGS and 
DOE-related mineral and geothermal resource assessments of the region. 
Stakeholders for this project include  the USGS Geology, Minerals, Energy, and Geophysics 
Science Center, the DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy, the Nevada Bureau 
of Mines and Geology, Tribal Councils, and other state and federal offices. 
 
The project was managed by the designated USGS Point -of-Contact (POC) from the National 
Geospatial Technical Operations Center in Rolla, Missouri.  Chase Thompson was the Project 
Manager for the NV5 Geospatial GPSC3 contract (G16PC00016). Technical Liaisons from the 
USGS directed the survey planning and conducted reviews of the preliminary field data. 
 
 
Figure 2. The Shawnee Mtns. and Granite Springs Valley, Pershing County. 
 
Michael Hobbs, VP of Operations for EDCON-PRJ, managed the data acquisition, quality control, 
and communications with the USGS and DOE Technical Liaisons. Nick Anderson conducted the 
in-office quality control and processing of the data for EDCON -PRJ as well as the generation of 
the grids and databases used for daily reviews.  
 
A large number of individuals contributed their time, talent, focus and effort to the field acquisition 
of the GeoDAWN data.  Cloudstreet Flying Service, owned and operated by Joe Nance, conducted

Survey Planning 
 
The first set of preliminary boundaries for the GeoDAWN Project was  forwarded by the USGS 
staff to NV5 Geospatial in late September of 2020. The RFP and an initial Task Order was issued 
on November 10, 2020 and a Technical Exchange was conducted on November 20, 2020.  
During the Technical Exchange with USGS and DOE scientists and staff, agreements were reached 
on the type of aircraft to be utilized, the use and characteristics of hybrid drape surfaces, and with 
excluding areas over Tribal Lands within the survey. The NV5 Team submitted its final Technical 
Proposal in January of 2021 and the Notice to Proceed was received in March of 2021. 
 
 
Figure 3. The Four GeoDAWN Acquisition Blocks. 
 
Given the extremely large size of the project, it was decided to divide the survey into four separate 
acquisition blocks, each with an independent base of operations at the towns  which became the 
name for each block. Acquisition was planned to begin at the northernmost block in the fall of 


6 
 
2021 and proceed south as weather permitted and then to return in 2022 to complete the unfinished 
blocks. 
The USGS staff coordinated efforts to contact Tribes within the survey area for permission to 
overfly their lands during survey operations. Many tribes agreed to having the survey include their 
lands, but several did not. Blocks of excluded airspace were generated to honor this request and 
were incorporated into the flight navigation planning for the aircraft. The largest of these areas can 
be seen  as red polygons in the image above (Figure 3.). 
The GeoDAWN Project included two primary areas. Area 1 is located southwest of Tonopah and 
was incorporated into the Tonopah acquisition block. Area 1 (The Clayton Valley) was designated 
to be a high-density survey with primary flight traverse lines flown on a N 90° E heading at 200-
meter spacings and tie lines to be flown on a N180°E heading at 2,000- meter spacings with a 
hybrid, pre-planned drape of 100 meters over basins and 150 meters over mountain ranges (Figure 
4.).   
 
 
Figure 4. The Layout for Area 1 (Clayton Valley) with 100-meter drape areas shown in gray. 
 
The rest of the GeoDAWN survey area (Area 2) was designated to have primary flight traverse 
lines flown on a N 90° E heading at 400-meter spacings and tie lines flown on a N180°E heading 
at 4,000-meter spacings, with a hybrid, pre -planned drape of 150 meters over the basins and 200 
meters over the mountain ranges. Minor adjustments to the survey areas were made in consultation 


7 
 
with the USGS Technical Liaisons to square up boundary lines, improve the drape surface in areas 
of high relief, and eliminate short line segments without tie line intersections.  
The pre-planned drape surfaces were generated using proprietary software that incorporate s the 
digital terrain model, the required drape height, and the flight characteristics of the survey aircraft 
(survey speed, vertical acceleration, and rate of climb) to produce a hyperbolically- smoothed 
digital surface that allow s close terrain following within a safe -flight envelope for the survey 
aircraft. A climb/dive angle of 22 degrees was used for both Area 1 and Area 2 flight planning. 
Data Acquisition 
 
Data acquisition for the GeoDAWN Project required 142 days of fixed- wing flights and 22 days 
of helicopter flights. A total of 149,030 kilometers of magnetic and radiometric data was acquired 
over 1,055 primary flight lines and 83 tie lines (Figure 5.).  
 
 
Figure 5. Magnetic Intensity Maps Showing Monthly Acquisition Progress. 
 
Survey operations were originally planned to begin on August 1, 2021, but numerous large 
wildfires in the Sierra Nevada Mountains created unsafe haze conditions that persisted throughout 
the months of August, September, and much of October (Figure 6.).

Figure 6. The NOAA Air Quality Map for August 26, 2021. 
 
The wildfires finally abated mid -October, and the Cessna -180 fixed- wing crew mobilized to 
Winnemucca on October 30 and established the initial base of operations at the Winnemucca 
Municipal Airport. After conducting  compensation flights , equipment calibrations and testing, 
and the establishment of a radiometric control line, data acquisition commenced on November 1 
and continued until December 7, when an early, heavy snow blanketed the entire GeoDAWN 
survey area.  
 
The crew was not able to reposition to the southern portion of the GeoDAWN project because 
heavy snow had collected on the higher ranges even 300 kilometers to the south. As a result, 
acquisition was halted for the balance of the year and the crew mobilized to the Salton Sea area 
and acquired data for the GeoFLIGHT project during the winter. A total of 21,546 line-kilometers 
of magnetic and radiometric data within the Winnemucca Block w as acquired during the fall of 
2021. 
 
The Cloudstreet crew re- mobilized to Winnemucca on May 14, 2022 with a different aircraft, a 
Cessna Turbo 206, and re -commenced acquisition on May 15. They completed the 62,530 
kilometers of the Winnemucca Block in July and relocated their base of operations to the Fallon 
Municipal Airport. At each of these  operational bases, a new radiometric control line was 
established and was flown each day prior to data collection.

Survey Methodology 
 
Aircraft Compensations: 
Prior to the commencement of survey operations,  magnetic compensation and calibration routines 
were conducted to generate coefficients used during processing of the survey data. The pilot s 
climbed to approx. 1,500 meters (4,900 ft.) above terrain and flew a square pattern in the four 
cardinal directions at a high altitude to suppress geologic effects on the readings. The pattern was 
flown twice, once “flat” as if conducting survey operations and once while performing moderate 
roll, pitch, and yaw maneuvers.  
Several compensation routines were conducted during the GeoDAWN Project : One with the 
Cessna-180 at the beginning of survey operations, one with the Cessna -206 when it arrived at 
Winnemucca and another when it was fitted with a new engine, and one was conducted with the 
Jet Ranger helicopter when it began operations on the Tonopah Block. The data from the flux-gate 
magnetometers were processed to generate X, Y and Z -axis coefficients used to compensate for 
heading and maneuver error during survey operations. 
 
Radar Altimeter Calibrations: 
Radar altimeter calibrations were performed on each aircraft to calculate the coefficients to convert 
mVolts as supplied by the on- board altimeter to meters  for the database . Identical FreeFlight 
Systems TRA3000 radar altimeters were mounted on all aircraft utilized o n the GeoDAWN 
Project. Each radar altimeter calibration included coefficients generated at a range of altitudes from 
60 meters to 450 meters.  
 
Cosmic Calibration: 
Radiometric data were acquired during the compensation routines for the purpose of determining 
the cosmic background and aircraft background counts to be used in the processing of spectrometer 
data. The cosmic background data were acquired for 20 minutes at an elevation of 1,500 meters 
(4,900 ft.) AGL for each aircraft. 
 
Sensitivity and Height Attenuation Calibration: 
The radiometric sensitivity and attenuation calibrations were conducted in conjunction with the 
radar altimeter calibrations. Data were collected during a series of passes over the same traverse 
at altitudes ranging from 60 meters to 450 meters to determine the height attenuation coefficients. 
 
 

13 
 
Daily Test Line Survey: 
Radiometric control line s were established in proximity to each of the bases of operation. Data 
were collected at survey drape height each day prior to data acquisition. A minimum of 200 
seconds of data were recorded each traverse. The control line data is used to correct for daily 
variations in temperature, humidity, and ground- level radon concentrations. A spreadsheet of the 
daily control line data was included for review in the materials uploaded daily to the USGS and is 
included in its entirety with the deliverables for this project (Appendix B.). 
 
Daily Spectrometer Resolution Tests: 
Thorium resolution tests were conducted each day prior to takeoff. First, the radiometric system 
was allowed to warm up for 10 minutes, then 5 minutes of data were recorded in a defined location, 
followed by 5 minutes of data collected with a Thorium source placed beneath the aircraft . A 
spreadsheet of these daily sensitivity tests was included in the materials uploaded daily to the 
USGS and is included with the deliverables for this project (Appendix C.). 
 
Calculated Radar and GPS Position Corrections: 
Instead of providing data from a barometric altimeter and in addition to radar altimeter coverage, 
we acquired and provide “calculated radar” elevations for the aircraft. The calculated radar values 
are generated by taking the NAD83/NAVD88 elevation values and subtracting the corresponding 
values from the  USGS 3DEP Elevation.  
We applied Precise Point Positioning corrections to the on-board GPS data, which results in GPS 
elevation accuracies at the sub -meter level. We utilize Inertial Explorer software, developed by 
Novatel, Inc., to accomplish the post -flight GPS corrections. P recise Point Positioning is similar 
to differential corrections made with a GPS base station  and is much more accurate when flight 
line lengths are as long as were encountered in this survey.  
 
Quality Control Measures: 
The data from each flight were reviewed three times by separate individuals. The data were first 
reviewed by the pilot and crew immediately after each flight.  Then the data were downloaded 
from the onboard computer and uploaded via ftp site to EDCON -PRJ’s offices in Lakewood, 
Colorado for formal quality review.  
As a second check, Michael Hobbs, Operations Manager for EDCON-PRJ, would then download 
the data and review for quality and completeness. Finally, in Lakewood, each flight’s data were 
checked for quality by Nick Anderson, Airborne Data Processor for EDCON -PRJ, and then 
compensated and corrected for diurnal activity using data collected from the base station 
magnetometer. The data were reviewed for flight line accuracy, GPS resolution, radar altimeter 
positioning, magnetometer noise levels, and spectrometer count levels, tuning, and resolution.

Daily acquisition updates were forwarded via e -mail to the USGS and DOE staff . Two Geosoft 
databases, one for magnetic data and one for radiometric data, were updated daily and posted to 
the USGS folder on EDCON-PRJ’s ftp site for review.  
The data quality was very high during all phases of this survey. Diurnal activity during the survey

line patches.

Safety Plan 
 
All flight operations were conducted with safety and environmental awareness as the number one 
priority.   
 
Figure 12. The EDCON-PRJ IAGSA Certificate. 
 
EDCON-PRJ is a member of the International Airborne Geophysics Safety Association and 
subscribes to its safety procedures and policies.  Our safety plan for each project includes: 
 
1. A thorough review of the survey area by staff and pilots for risk assessment, drape 
considerations, and public safety. 
 
2. Daily inspection of equipment and aircraft for performance and operational safety. 
 
3. Daily flight and safety planning were conducted with the crew prior to takeoff.  
 
4. Real-time on -board weather radar and surface conditions reporting through satellite 
service. 
 
5. An Emergency Response Plan (EMP) was completed prior to the commencement of survey 
operations and included contact information for pilot and crew, local response teams and 
authorities. Local authorities were notified of the location and extent of survey operations 
prior to their commencement.  
 
6. Real time tracking of the survey aircraft and the chase vehicle through a satellite location 
service was conducted throughout survey operations. 
 
Prior to each flight, the pilot performed a complete pre-flight inspection of the aircraft, including 
control surfaces, fuel contamination testing, communications equipment, geophysical equipment, 
and electrical systems. The pilot and crew then reviewed the flight plan for the day and discussed 
any potential safety issues due to terrain, obstacles, wind, and weather.

Gamma Ray Spectrometers: 
 
Nuvia Dynamics Advanced Gamma Ray Spectrometers (AGRSs) were utilized by both the fixed-
wing and helicopter platforms for the GeoDAWN Project  (Figure 16.). The fixed-wing system

The helicopter system consisted of 16.8 liters of downward- looking NaI detectors and a 4.2 liter 
upward-looking detector.  The AGRS systems  feature a 512-channel microprocessor, automatic 
calibration, and tuning using natural background elements.  
 
 
 
Figure 16. The Nuvia Dynamics Advanced Gamma Ray Spectrometer System. 
 
The spectrometers were calibrated prior to the start of survey operations and were tested for 
sensitivity and resolution prior to each day’ s flights. Data was logged over a control line each 
morning enroute to the survey area. The daily and control line data and resolution tests are attached 
to this report as (Appendix B.) and (Appendix C.).

Global Positioning Systems: 
 
Novatel PowerPak7 dual-band, geodetic-quality GPS receiver s were installed in  all aircraft and 
supplied navigation positioning as well as geophysical data points. WAAS DGPS corrections were 
utilized, providing sub-meter horizontal and vertical positioning (2D-RMS 95% probability).  The 
Novatel Receiver has a proven accuracy using WAAS of +/ - 0.6 m. GPS data were recorded at 5 
Hz during all survey operations. Precise Point Positioning (PPP) corrections were employed post-
flight to increase positional accuracy even further. 
 
 
Fluxgate Compensation Magnetometers: 
 
An Applied Physics Systems Model 539 High Speed Digital 3- axis Fluxgate Magnetometer was 
used to provide compensation data to the fixed-wing aircraft during survey operations. The Model 
539 records X, Y, and Z- axis data at 30 Hz. 
 
A Billingsley Model TFM100G2 Triaxial Fluxgate Magnetometer provided compensation data to 
the Bell Jet Ranger during survey operations. The Model TFM 100G2 was recording data at 50 
Hz. 
 
 
Radar Altimeters: 
 
FreeFlight Systems TRA-3000 radar altimeters were mounted on the bottom of each of the aircraft 
and provided ground clearance information to the pilot as well as aircraft elevation positions to the 
geophysical data stream.  The TRA-3000 Radar Altimeter provides precise altitude information at 
10 Hz, with a digital recording resolution of 0.25 ft. 
 
 
Survey Navigation Systems: 
 
An Ag -Nav Platinum three -dimensional survey and aircraft guidance system was utilized to 
provide pilot guidance for the fixed-wing aircraft. The system provides the pilot with cross -track 
X-Y guidance and a vertical lightbar supplies Z -axis positions relative to the pre -planned drape 
surface. 
 
The Bell Jet Ranger utilized a Nuvia Dynamics Airborne Geophysical Information System (AGIS) 
for survey navigation. Three -axis guidance is supplied by a flat screen display mounted on the 
instrument dash of the helicopter. The display also allows the pilot to monitor the status of all on-
board geophysical systems, so that a geophysical operator is not necessary.

permanently after leveling per the USGS request.

    4. Leveling and Micro-leveling 
 
Mis-ties at line intersections were calculated and adjusted to minimize mis -tie errors.  
Initial leveling adjustments were completed using a DC level adjustment to compensate 
for long wavelength diurnal effects. The average intersection mis -tie before DC 
adjustment was 13.82 nT; after DC adjustment, the average mis-tie was 3.12 nT. After 
micro-leveling the average mis-tie was 0.57 nT. 
 
After DC-leveling, a slight strike filter was applied to the magnetic grid to remove any 
line-pulls, which was then used as a leveling surface in micro-leveling. 
 
The DC-leveled data showed line some line-oriented anomalies. A strike filter removed 
these minor line pulls.  Then, the DC -leveled data were subtracted from the strike 
filtered data to make a difference channel. A new leveled magnetics channel was 
created by subtracting the filtered difference from the DC-leveled mag.  This non-linear 
adjustment helped to maintain valid detail with minimal filtering.   
 
After micro-leveling a small number of tie-lines still were not at the correct level due 
to lower elevations than the corresponding flight lines. As a result, the tie-lines in these 
locations showed anomalies that were not present on the flight lines. To get these 
intersections leveled, the tie -lines were forced to the flight -line levels by linear 
interpolation, then micro-leveled again, which removed anomalies caused by the linear 
interpolation.

 C. Spectrometer Data 
 
Spectrometer data was processed using Geosoft Praga 4 software. The processing 
workflow followed Sections 1-6 below: 
 
 
1.  Filtering  
 
All spectrometer data had NASVD spectra smoothing applied to it. 
 
 
2. Reduction to Standard Temperature and Pressure 
  
A temperature and relative humidity sensor was mounted in each aircraft and recorded 
data during all survey traverses. The radar altimeter data were reduced to effective 
height in meters using the temperature and pressure data millibars. 
 
 
3. Dead Time Correction  
 
The data were corrected for dead-time at 10 milliseconds.

Radon removal is one of the most difficult tasks to be done when processing 
radiometric data. Radon concentrations vary widely from day- to-day due to 
fluctuations in temperature, pressure, humidity and atmospheric inversions. Although 
utilizing counts from the upward -looking crystal are the preferred method of radon 
removal, the process is not perfect.

7. Daily Resolution Test 
   
The AGRS spectrometer is a self-calibrating system that calibrates itself to Thorium 
peaks as it logs data. A Thorium sensitivity test was conducted each day prior to 
takeoff. First, the radiometric system was allowed to warm up for 10 minutes, then 
five minutes of data were recorded in a defined location, followed by five minutes 
of data collected with a Thorium source placed beneath the aircraft. The data were 
checked each day to make sure the resolution of the spectra remained well within 
specification (Appendix C.).  
 
 
8.  Daily Control Line  
 
Radiometric control lines were established near each base of operations. Data were 
collected at survey drape height each day prior to data acquisition. A minimum of 
200 seconds of data were recorded each traverse. Data were collected along this same 
traverse daily and checked for consistency (Appendix B).
