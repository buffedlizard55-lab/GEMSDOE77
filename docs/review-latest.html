Why H33 might have received the owner's 0.2778 — the limit of inference

Hash-pinned H33 byte forensics record a 37,654-dot raster derived by deleting 6,436 dots within 200 m of the catalogue from a 44,090-dot predecessor without adding dots. The official metric gives truth credit through each truth pixel's maximum nearby triangular-kernel prediction, but each additional predicted pixel adds to false-positive mass unless it increases useful coverage. Under α=0.2, β=0.8, let T be total maximum truth credit, S predicted mass, M prediction mass discounted by distance to the nearest truth, and K truth count. The score is T/[0.2(T+S−M)+0.8K]. For a new binary dot with nearest-truth weight k and total added coverage ΔT, improvement requires ΔT > 0.2·DTI·(ΔT + 1 − k). The frequently repeated k > 0.2·DTI criterion is only the special case ΔT = k. One dot may cover several truth pixels, while another adds no new truth coverage at all. Sparse spacing and near-catalogue pruning plausibly improve coverage per predicted mass; those 6,436 deleted dots may instead contain useful hidden-fault coverage. Neither the owner-supplied score sequence nor the page's projection proves causation or establishes an optimal spacing. Mathematical correction and tests.

To beat the owner-reported 0.2778—or the observed public first-place 0.4413—requires an independent, well-localized fault signal that improves paired spatially blocked hide-and-recover DTI against a fold-rebuilt incumbent, with every catalogue-based predictor restricted to visible faults, pixel-exact masking and feature-alone leakage canaries. Even that is only a proxy: expert-labeled new faults and private/final rounds can differ from withheld known faults. We cannot honestly guarantee a leaderboard score or create a passing submission while the current universal witness rule holds.
